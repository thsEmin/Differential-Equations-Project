01 — THE PROJECT, EXPLAINED

From a physical rule
to a quick prediction.

A guided tour of the equation, the simulator, the model, and what this experiment can—and cannot—tell us.

THE QUESTION

Can a model trained to respect a heat equation predict a temperature map faster than a step-by-step solver?

Try the experiment ↑
01 / THE PHYSICS

What does the heat equation say?

At each point on a plate, temperature changes as heat flows in from warmer neighbors, flows out toward cooler neighbors, and arrives from a heater. Our simplified two-dimensional equation is:

∂T/∂t = α (∂²T/∂x² + ∂²T/∂y²) + q(x, y)

T temperature · t time · α how readily heat spreads · q heat supplied by the heater

In everyday words: a spot warms when heat flows into it or the heater adds energy. The plate edges are held at room temperature.

02 / THE SOLVER

How does the traditional method work?

We divide the plate into a grid of 48 × 48 small squares. Starting at room temperature, the program repeatedly estimates heat flow between neighboring squares, then advances by a tiny time step. After many steps, we have a temperature map.

03 / THE MODEL

How does machine learning fit in?

We first used the simulator to create examples of plates with different heater positions, strengths and times. A small neural network learned a mapping from those settings to a temperature map.

While it learned, it received two kinds of feedback: “Does your map match the simulator’s example?” and “Do your predicted temperatures change in a way that follows the heat equation?” The second check is the physics-informed part.

TRAINING FEEDBACKExample mismatch + 0.01 × heat-equation mismatchThe equation weight was selected in our validation experiment.

After training, prediction is one forward pass through the network. It does not march through each time step like the solver.

04 / THE FAIR TEST

How do we compare them fairly?

Both methods use the same heater position, strength, time, grid and boundary conditions. We time each on the device running this page, and compare every grid cell against the solver’s output.

RMSE (root mean square error) summarizes how far temperatures differ. Zero means an exact match; lower is closer. Hot-area RMSE checks the hottest part separately, where an overall average might hide mistakes.

Timing is a single local-device measurement repeated several times. It does not include the model’s earlier training cost, and the result depends on the browser and device.

05 / LIMITS & NEXT

What should we conclude?

This is a simplified computer experiment—not a calibrated simulation of a particular metal. The model is only trained for times between 0 and 0.3 and heater settings inside the training range. It may be less accurate outside them.

A faster prediction is useful only if its error is acceptable. Our website shows both values together so speed does not hide accuracy. The numerical solver is still the reference for this project.
